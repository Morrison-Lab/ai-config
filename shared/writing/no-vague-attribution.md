# No vague attribution

Name the real source of a claim.
When the draft has none, go and find one.
That is the fix, not dropping the claim (Ezra, 2026-11-06).
Never invent a vague attribution to give a claim the look of support.

Phrases that do this:

- "instructors often observe"
- "it is well known"
- "experts agree"
- "studies show"
- "in practice, people find"
- "it is commonly said"

Each one tells the reader that a source exists and withholds it.

Three rules follow.

- **A source exists:** cite it, with a locator, through the repository's bibliography ([`citations`](citations.md)).
  If the claim came from a lecturer, a slide deck or an earlier version of the text, say so by name ([`visible-attributions`](visible-attributions.md)).
- **No source in the draft:** search for one first: the project's books and bibliography, then the literature.
  Cite what you find, with a locator.
- **A real search finds none:** state the claim in the author's voice and own it ("I expect", "we find in the example below").
  Never cut the claim only because it lacks a source.

A reviewer's "this claim has no citation" asks for the source.
Softening the wording, hedging the claim, or swapping in a vague attribution does not answer it.
Find the source.
Only if a real search finds none, keep the claim and say it is the author's judgment.

Before adding a hedge to satisfy a review note, ask where the claim came from.
Trace it to its origin first (a slide, a paper, a prior version of the page).
The origin is usually the right citation.

Case: lds#935 (2026-10-14).
A review bot flagged an uncited claim from a lecturer's slides.
The first fix wrote "instructors often observe", which hid that the claim was one lecturer's.
The right fix names that lecturer and the deck, as the first rule above says.
